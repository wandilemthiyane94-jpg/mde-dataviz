Who owns the flood?

South Africa's flood response is split across three spheres of government and eight hand-offs. No one owns the whole process, so the risk lands on whoever cannot push back: families moved onto riverbanks and residents who never get a warning in their language.

1 · The South African system

Eight links, three spheres, no owner

Each column is one link in the chain from forecast to permanent home. Each box is the body that holds that link in eThekwini. The dashed lines are hand-offs: nobody owns them, and they are where the failures happen. The families at the bottom are not in any box.

  1. 1Warning to residents. Official warnings are in English. At the sites where people died, a median of 3.1% speak English as a first language. Only 3 of 72 flood events have a resident speaking about the warning in the coverage.
  2. 2Money and land are split. The province funds and owns the shelters; the city has to find land. In 2022/23, R360m went unspent and none of 2,224 planned flood units were built.
  3. 3Ratepayers veto; the riverbank remains. Umhlanga, Shallcross and Montclair groups blocked sites. 41% of land is private and 18% is Ingonyama Trust land. What's left is the riverbank and the canal edge.
  4. 4"Temporary" becomes permanent. The emergency programme says permanent-structure norms "will not apply". The city has built 71 transit camps since 2009; Isipingo has lasted 14 years. The backlog is 469,500 households, and the city calls moving flood victims up the list "que-jumping".
  5. 5The warning goes nowhere. The Auditor-General flagged "some units… built on unsuitable land" in August 2022. No one owned the fix, and the camp flooded in February 2025. The mayor said it "is related mainly to the size of the storm event".
2 · The system that works

One owner, with powers, answerable to residents

This model is drawn from five relocations that moved people out of danger: Grantham, Overdiepse, Gramalote, Iwanuma and Vunidogoloa. One public body holds every link from housing money to the final outcome. It has the power to secure land, one budget and legal authority. Residents sit on the siting and design decisions, and they come out with title or permanent tenancy.

The success cases are relocations. None of them tested the warning links (forecast, passing the warning on, evacuation). Extending one owner to those links is this project's thesis, shown with a dotted line.

3 · South Africa is not alone

Systems like South Africa's fail the same way

In five other places the state relocated people and they stayed exposed. Each has the same structure: responsibility is split, the site is the cheapest or leftover land, the warning existed and wasn't binding, and after the flood the blame went to the weather or the residents.

4 · The test

What separates failure from success

We scored all 11 cases on ten structural factors, then compared how often each factor appears in the 6 failures and the 5 successes. Wealth, courts and corruption don't separate them. Ownership, residents' say, tenure and binding hazard rules do.

What doesn't separate them: wealth (Fiji and Colombia succeeded; Haiti failed despite large amounts of international money), courts (no case in either group was forced by a court), and whether the housing programme was permanent (Kasiglahan and Chennai were permanent housing programmes). The less obvious finding is that the successes repeated the failure for their own residents with no standing. Grantham excluded renters and people without insurance. Overdiepse pushed out tenant farmers, and only 8 of 17 families stayed. Gramalote deliberately turned its 40% renters into owners, and it worked for them.

5 · The systems map

Two loops

Failure reinforces itself. Because there is no consequence, nothing changes before the next flood. In the success loop, the owner is judged on whether people are safe years later, which pulls every decision toward the hazard-free site.

6 · The hierarchy of the problem

Ownership at the root, implementation below

  1. L1Government owns the whole process
    One accountable body from warning to permanent home, judged on "safe years later", with residents given a real say in the decisions. Present in 5 of 5 successes; absent in 6 of 6 failures.
  2. L2Powers that make ownership real
    Land, one budget and legal authority. Grantham used a state reconstruction declaration; Gramalote used Colombia's Law 1523, Article 73 to buy or expropriate land. In eThekwini this is the hard, political level: private land, Ingonyama Trust land and ratepayer vetoes.
  3. L3Design rules the owner sets
    A measure of success based on outcomes, not units delivered. No emergency unit inside the 1:100 floodline. A land bank before the next flood. A guaranteed, time-limited path from temporary to permanent housing.
  4. L4Implementation
    Scoring sites on hazard first (Gramalote weighted stability 30 of 100 points), build quality, electricity, livelihoods, and warnings in residents' languages. These are fixable once someone owns them.
  5. L5Symptoms
    Blaming the rain, "temporary" lasting 14 years, repeat displacement, deaths. This is the level the media sees.

Corruption (the R32m probe found "not a single house was built"), capacity gaps (58% of water and sanitation infrastructure posts are vacant) and fragmented budgets cut across every level. They make failure worse, but fixing them without L1 doesn't change the outcome.

Method and caveats

We scored 11 cases on ten factors; codes are our reading of sourced findings (data/relocation_rootcause_matrix.csv, built by methods/scripts/build_relocation_rootcause.ps1).

  • Small, outcome-selected sample. Cases were chosen because of how they turned out, so the clean separation overstates how predictive the factors are. Read it as a pattern, not a statistical test.
  • Imperfect successes. Grantham excluded non-owners, Gramalote took six years and was a landslide rather than a flood, and Vunidogoloa's promised infrastructure was never built.
  • Weaker evidence. Beledweyne rests on a submitted manuscript that may not yet be peer-reviewed. Some quotes come from search snippets and are tagged in the raw JSON.
  • Consistent with other research. The Bower et al. (2023) comparison of 14 flood relocations also found community-driven relocations did better.
