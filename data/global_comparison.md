# South Africa's last-mile failure in global context

**Sources:**
- `l2_comprehension_evidence.json` — 24 research findings on second-language (L2) comprehension
- `global_language_cases.json` — 26 cases where the language of a warning mattered
- `global_lastmile_cases.json` — 19 disasters, 2013–2025, coded against the failure points listed in the table below

**Labels:** **Finding** means what the sources show. **Reading** means interpretation.

---

## 1. Does receiving a warning in a second language make it less effective?

**Finding.** The evidence points the same way, but most of it comes from lab studies:

| Mechanism | Evidence | Strength |
|---|---|---|
| **Stress hurts L2 more than L1.** Stress slowed L2 readers but not L1 readers. | Rai, Loschky & Harris 2015, *J. Educ. Psych.* 107(2); Xia et al. 2025 | Moderate (replicated in the lab) |
| **Noise hurts non-native listeners more, and the gap grows as conditions get worse.** This is directly relevant to radio, loudhailers and sirens heard over rain. | Garcia Lecumberri, Cooke & Cutler 2010, *Speech Communication* 52 (review) | Strong |
| **Emotional words produce a weaker body response in L2**, so less urgency is felt. | Harris et al. 2003; Caldwell-Harris 2015; Pavlenko 2012 | Moderate–strong |
| **The same hazard is judged less risky in a foreign language.** | Hadjichristidis et al. 2015, *JEP: Applied* 21(2) | Single study, contested |
| **Framing biases shrink in a foreign language (the foreign-language effect).** | Keysar et al. 2012; meta-analyses Circi 2021, Del Maschio 2022 | Real but small; some replications fail |
| **Translated warning terms lose meaning and urgency.** 66% of English speakers understood "tornado watch", against 38% of Spanish speakers given the official translation. | Trujillo-Falcón et al. 2022, *BAMS* 103(12) | Strong (field survey) |
| **Colour and level scales are ambiguous even in a person's first language.** | Tang 2022, *Applied Linguistics* | Moderate |

**Caveats.**
- Most participants learned their L2 late, in a lab setting. South Africans often learn English early at school, so the effects may be smaller.
- The stress studies mostly show slower understanding, not large drops in accuracy.
- **No study has tested whether people understand SAWS warnings, or translated warning terms, in isiZulu, isiXhosa, Tshivenda, Xitsonga or Sepedi.** That gap is where this project can contribute.

**Reading.** The effects compound. A warning in English, which is the L2 at the places where people died (median English first-language share: 3.1%), is:
- expressed as a colour or level that is ambiguous even in a person's first language,
- received at night, under stress,
- heard over noise.

Every one of those conditions is where research finds L2 comprehension degrades most.

## 2. Language failures elsewhere (26 cases)

**The closest parallel is Mozambique.** Only about half of Mozambicans speak Portuguese. After Cyclone Idai in 2019, 33% of people surveyed in Beira said they lacked information in a language they understand; more than 600 people died. For Cyclone Freddy in 2023, warnings went out over community radio in local languages and a free phone line, and fewer than 200 people died.

**Other cases:**
- **USA, 2017–18 wildfires:** the California State Auditor found none of three counties warned in any language other than English. SB 160 (2019) followed.
- **New York, Hurricane Ida 2021:** nearly all 11 people who died in basements were Asian immigrants; alerts had gone out only in English and Spanish.
- **Philippines, Typhoon Haiyan 2013:** "storm surge" was not understood, and survivors called it a tsunami. Warnings now pair the term with the Filipino "daluyong ng bagyo" and give surge heights.
- **Australia, Northern Territory 2019:** English-only cyclone updates caused panic at Ngukurr.
- **Canada 2019–22:** 0 of 1,307 broadcast alerts were in any Indigenous language.
- **Pakistan 2022:** a flood survivor said "Urdu is difficult for us to understand."

**What works: trusted local people speaking the warning.**
- **Bangladesh:** the Cyclone Preparedness Programme (CPP) uses 55,000–76,000 volunteers with megaphones and a three-flag code, speaking Bangla.
- **Odisha, Cyclone Fani 2019:** loudspeaker warnings in local languages and 1.2 million evacuated. The 1999 super cyclone killed about 10,000.
- **Elsewhere:** Yolŋu Radio in Australia's Northern Territory; young volunteers in Kenya translating into Turkana.

**South Africa's equivalents:**
- Quarry Road's warning system, run over WhatsApp and whistles.
- The 88% trust in isiXhosa community radio found in the Walmer Airport Valley survey.

**Pattern: deaths, then an inquiry, then a language policy.** Examples are California SB 160, the US federal rule requiring alert templates in 13 languages by 2028, New York's basement alerts in 14 languages, and Japan's "Easy Japanese". South Africa has had the deaths (435+ in April 2022 alone), but no inquiry into the warning chain or its language has been found.

## 3. Failure points: South Africa compared with 19 global disasters

The 19 global disasters (2013–2025) are Ahr 2021, Derna 2023, Valencia 2024, Kerr County 2025, Zhengzhou 2021, Western Japan 2018, Haiyan 2013, Idai 2019, Freddy 2023, Kenya 2024, Nigeria 2022, Pakistan 2022, Kerala 2018, Rio Grande do Sul 2024, Gabrielle 2023, Lismore 2022, Lahaina 2023, and two success comparators, Fani 2019 and Amphan 2020.

| Failure point | Global (true / 19) | South Africa evidence |
|---|---|---|
| **F7** No clear action or destination | **13** | Colour and level alerts with no "go here" instruction. Quarry Road's nearest shelter is 5 km away. Amnesty found residents "largely unaware" of warning systems. |
| **F11** The forecast worked; the last mile failed | **13** | Jan 2026: Red Level 10 was accurate — "Technologically, the system worked. Socially, it faltered." Also Durban 2017 and the Sep 2023 Orange Level 9. |
| **F1** People didn't understand what the alert meant | 11 | Limpopo 2026: "very few had an idea of what this meant." In KZN in 2026, a Groundwork representative: "What is a level 4?" |
| **F3** The warning didn't reach people (channel failure) | 11 | No cell broadcast, and the NDMC system is opt-in. Walmer survey: only 90 of 258 phone owners had ever received a warning on their phone. |
| **F5** Officials say warnings were sent; residents say they weren't received | 11 | April 2022: the city had "probably" sent alerts; residents said none arrived. Walmer: 210 of 270 had never received a warning. |
| **F4** The warning came late or overnight | 10 | Lamontville 2025 around 1am; Thuli's account, 2am in 2022; Mdantsane 2022 "without warning". |
| **F8** Distrust or distance between the state and residents | 6 | Officials enter informal settlements only with armed guards (Amnesty). |
| **F2** The warning's language doesn't match the population's | **3** | **Structural in South Africa:** English at death sites where the median English first-language share is 3.1%. |
| **F10** Blame shifted to where residents live or to climate | 3 (14 unclear) | **Measured in South Africa:** siting blamed in 86%, and climate in 71%, of mass-casualty events. In Lamontville the state had chosen the site. |
| **F6** Warning fatigue from false alarms | 2 | KZN 2026: false alarms breed complacency (Groundwork). |
| **F9** Relief only once a disaster is big enough | **0 (19 unclear)** | **Measured in South Africa:** relief only after a "major incident" is declared; the rising-toll pattern appears in 0 of 25 small events. |
| **S1** Success through community intermediaries | 7 | Quarry Road (no drownings in April 2022); isiXhosa community radio in Walmer. |

**Finding.**
1. **Most of South Africa's failure is the global norm.** The four most common global failures are F7 (13), F11 (13), F1 (11) and F3 (11), and South Africa shows all four. So does F5 (also 11): officials saying warnings were sent while residents say none arrived, a dispute that happens everywhere and is settled by data almost nowhere.
2. **Language (F2) looks rare in the global sample, 3 of 19, because most of those countries are linguistically homogeneous.** Where a population is split by language (Mozambique, Pakistan, the Philippines, Canada, California), language failure appears every time. South Africa, with 12 official languages and English-only warnings, belongs in that second group.
3. **The biggest gap is in the global record, not in South Africa.** Official inquiries almost never record F9 (relief only for large disasters) or F10 (blame moved to location or climate): 0 and 3 of 19, with most cases unclear. This project measures both from media and response data. That is a methodological contribution: inquiries examine the warning system, while this project also examines how the aftermath is explained.

## 4. What changed after other disasters, and where South Africa stands

| Change | Where it happened | South Africa status |
|---|---|---|
| Cell broadcast that needs no sign-up | Germany 2023 (after Ahr); Brazil Dec 2025; the US and EU already have it | None nationally; the NDMC system is opt-in |
| Pre-approved alert templates in multiple languages | US federal rule (13 languages + ASL, by 2028); Japan (15 languages); India's national alert system | None found. SAWS warnings are English-only; eThekwini's isiZulu warnings are ad hoc |
| Standing volunteer network for warnings | Bangladesh CPP; Odisha; Mozambique's ~1,500 local disaster risk committees; Malawi anticipatory-action villages (~20% more likely to receive, trust and act on warnings) | Quarry Road pilot. Not yet copied to eThekwini's 180 high-risk settlements; 2 more sites under way |
| Inquiry into the warning chain with legal force | Germany (Rhineland-Palatinate inquiry); NSW Lismore (28 recommendations); NZ Hawke's Bay ("set up good people to fail"); Texas legislative committee; Valencia court case | No inquiry into warnings or their language found. Amnesty calls for an inquiry into the Lamontville relocation. |
| Household surveys measuring whether people received warnings | Germany: 29–35% of residents received no warning | The Walmer Airport Valley survey is the only one found |

## 5. Five lessons for South Africa

1. **Every warning should say what to do and where to go**, not just give a colour. F7 is the most common failure worldwide: in Germany, 46% of people who were warned didn't know what to do.
2. **Warnings should be pre-written in local languages and spoken by trusted local people.** Translating on the day is too slow: an official testified that editing the Valencian wording helped delay Valencia's 2024 alert by about two hours (disputed).
3. **Receipt should be measured, not just sending.** The dispute between officials and residents (F5) can only be settled with delivery logs and surveys after the event.
4. **South Africa needs cell broadcast that requires no sign-up, with backups that don't depend on phones** (sirens, radio, door-to-door), and a plan for night-time events.
5. **Inquiries should examine the warning chain before where victims lived or the weather.** Everywhere a system improved, an inquiry with legal force came first.

## Caveats

- **Coding:** the global codings are judgements from cited evidence, and "unclear" means no source was found.
- **Unread reports:** three key reports couldn't be read in full: the Texas committee report, the Lahaina fire investigation and the Malawi Red Cross study.
- **Death tolls:** some are "widely reported" rather than taken from the cited page.
- **Lab evidence:** the second-language findings are mostly from lab studies, and whether they transfer to South Africa, where people learn English early, is untested.
- **Walmer figures:** they don't fully agree with each other in secondary summaries, so check the full text before quoting.
